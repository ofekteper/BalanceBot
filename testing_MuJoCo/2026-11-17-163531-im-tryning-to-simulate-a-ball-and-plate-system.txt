  3. Writing data.ctrl directly

  The three actuators are position servos named servo0, servo1 and servo2.
  data.ctrl[i] is the target angle in radians from neutral, clamped to ±45°.
  This skips the speed limit, so the servo jumps as fast as the model allows,
  which is faster than a real servo. ServoCommander avoids that.

  Things to know

  - Servo layout: servo 0 is on the +Y axis. Servos 1 and 2 follow at 120°
    steps.
  - Tilt sign convention: rx is tilt about X and ry is tilt about Y, matching
    plate_tilt(data).
  - Servo model: it's a position controller with SERVO_KP and SERVO_KV plus a
    torque limit. All three are guesses for an SG90-class servo.

  How the sensing works in the sim: a new file, ir_sensors.py
  (testing_MuJoCo/v2_cad/ir_sensors.py), simulates the light physics:
  - Each LED shines upward through the PMMA and scatters off the ball.
  - Each phototransistor collects the light that comes back within its viewing
    angle.
  - The plate's own surface reflection adds a constant background.
  - Readings come out as 10-bit ADC counts with noise, sampled at 40 Hz.
  - The ball position is estimated as the centroid of the readings above the
    no-ball level.

  What you see:
  - In the viewer: the phototransistors light up with their reading, and a green
    disc on the plate marks the estimated ball position.
  - In the control panel: a live 4×4 map of the counts, with servo 0 at the top.
    The true ball is an orange circle and the estimate a green cross, with the
    error shown in mm.

  Ambient Light Subtraction
  Room lighting (sunlight, artificial lights, screens) also emits infrared and
  would interfere with readings. BaBot eliminates this noise with a three-step

  Weighted Centroid: 16 Readings → 1 Position
  The 16 sensor values are converted into a single precise X,Y coordinate using
  a weighted centroid calculation. Each sensor's position is weighted by its
  brightness reading — the result is the mathematical center of the brightness
  distribution.

  X position = (sum of each sensor's X coordinate × brightness) ÷ (total
  brightness)
  Y position = (sum of each sensor's Y coordinate × brightness) ÷ (total
  brightness)
  Even when the ball sits between sensors, the weighted average determines the
  exact position with millimeter precision.

  How to read the BOM

  A BOM (bill of materials) is a semicolon-separated table with one row per part
  type. "Designator" lists the labels printed on the board, and "Manufacturer
  Part" is the part number you'd order. Yours has:
  - 21 × IR204/H60: 3 mm 940 nm IR LEDs (IR1–IR21), each with a 330 Ω resistor
    (R1–R21).
  - 16 × Würth 1540031NC6090: 3 mm phototransistors (Q1–Q16).
  - 1 × AO3400A MOSFET (Q17): switches all the LEDs on and off, with a 10 kΩ
    pull-down resistor (R22).
  - 1 × 20-pin flat-cable connector (U1) and its cable. This corrects what I
    told you before: U1 is the connector, not a multiplexer. The CD74HC4067
    multiplexer is on the main board, according to the firmware.

  What changed in the sensor model (ir_sensors.py
  (testing_MuJoCo/v2_cad/ir_sensors.py))

  - Real part sizes and angles: the parts are 3 mm, not 5 mm. The LED beam is
    ±25° and the phototransistor accepts ±30°, from the datasheets. I had
    guessed ±20° before.
  - Mirror-like reflection from the ping-pong ball, as the design description
    says, plus a little sideways light from the LED bodies.
  - Bright spot under the ball: the sensor directly beneath now reads about 1.5×
    its neighbours (603 against about 380). My earlier version had the
    opposite.
  - Firmware pipeline, line for line:
    - LEDs off, then LEDs on, then subtract.
    - Smoothing with α = 0.7.
    - Ball present when the highest reading minus the lowest is at least 350.
    - The cubic mirrored centroid.
    - 30 Hz, the firmware's rate, not the 40 Hz in your spec.

  Results

  - Ping-pong ball: about 7.5 mm average error inside the sensor grid and 4.4 mm
    outside it, with no false detections.
  - Why the inside error is larger: cubing the weights pulls the estimate toward
    the nearest sensor. That's how the firmware's algorithm behaves with these
    optics, so it's worth testing on the real board, which claims millimetre
    precision.
  - 20 mm plastic and steel balls: never detected. Their peaks (about 300 and
    170 counts) are below the firmware's 350 threshold, so the firmware only
    works with the ping-pong ball.
  - Speed: a sensor read takes about 9 ms per 33 ms cycle.

  Things to check on the real board

  - Sensor order: the flat cable is reverse-direction, so the multiplexer's
    channel order might be mirrored. I assumed channel i is Q(i+1). Enabling
    sendSerialData() in the firmware and moving the ball over one known sensor
    would confirm it.
  - Remaining guesses: the sideways LED light, the ball's reflectivity and the
    signal scale. The notes at the bottom of ir_sensors.py explain how to
    compare against the real board's serial output.

  Firmware facts for the next step (porting the PID)

  - Gains in the firmware: P 3.0, I 0.04, D 25, not the spec's 0.02 and 30. The
    output is also smoothed (α = 0.9).
  - Units: the PID works in sensor-pitch units (21.34 mm), and its output is
    roll and pitch in degrees.
  - IK matches our geometry exactly (19.0 / 62.3 mm radii, 50 / 39.2 mm arms, 65
    mm height).
  - Servo range: the firmware allows −10° to 85° crank angle, which is wider
    than the sim's ±45°.
  - Servo wiring: the firmware puts the legs at 0°, 120° and 240° and wires them
    to A, B and C in rotated order. To port it, I need to match those to the
    sim's legs.

● The firmware's balancing logic is now ported to the simulator. py run_sim.py
  opens in Balance mode: the ball is dropped 36 mm off-centre and the firmware
  brings it to the centre in about 1.5 s. I ran the real run_sim.py code for 2.4

  What was ported

  The new file is babot_firmware.py (testing_MuJoCo/v2_cad/babot_firmware.py).
  It follows main.ino closely, keeping the same variable names and order of
  operations:
  - The control loop (handleOnMode):
    - Skips 2 cycles when the ball first appears.
    - Runs PID on X and on Y, then smooths the output (α = 0.9).
    - If the ball is lost, holds the last tilt for 1 s, then resets and levels
      the plate.
  - pidControl: the exact formula, including the soft deadband on the derivative
    term (|vel|/(|vel|+0.001)).
  - movePlatform: the firmware's own approximate IK, not the simulator's exact
    one.
  - moveServos: crank angle clamped to −10°…85°, and whole degrees only, because
    Servo.write() takes an integer.
  - Timing: 30 Hz, with about 20 ms from reading the sensors until the servos
    get the new command (32 reads × 630 µs).

  To match the firmware, I also widened the sim's servo range from ±45° to the
  firmware's −10°…85° crank angle.

  Frame mapping: the only mapping where the firmware's signs push the ball the
  right way has firmware X = servo-0 direction and firmware Y = PCB +X. That's a
  mirror image of the PCB frame. On the real robot it must come from the
  flat-cable or multiplexer wiring; it's harmless because the platform is
  symmetric about servo 0. I checked it with a sign test (+roll makes the ball
  roll toward +X, as the PID expects), and the closed-loop tests confirm it.

  New panel controls (Balance mode)

  - P, I and D sliders, live, like the firmware's serial P…I…D… command, plus a
    "Default gains" button.
  - Setpoint X and Y in mm, drawn as a blue circle on the IR map.
  - Firmware ON/OFF, the equivalent of a single button press.
  - "Kick ball": a random push of about 0.15 m/s, for testing disturbances.
  - Live readout: whether the ball is detected, the firmware's centre estimate
    in sensor units, the PID roll and pitch output, and the integral terms.

  Why it stops short of an off-centre setpoint: cubing the weights pulls the
  firmware's position estimate toward the nearest sensor, so the firmware
  believes the ball has arrived. Watch for the same thing on the real robot: at
  the centre it's accurate, but between sensors it has a few mm of bias.

  The simulation is probably more forgiving than the real robot. The real one
  has servo backlash, plate flex and imperfect servo calibration, and the sensor
  optics are still estimates. Before you trust the PID tuning results, compare
  the real board's sendSerialData() output with the simulator. The notes at the
  bottom of ir_sensors.py explain how.
